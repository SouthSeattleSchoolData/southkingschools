    Youth suicide

    No agency publishes student suicide deaths by school or district. Small counts could identify a child, and publicity around clusters can raise risk for other students. In Washington, the suicide death rate for ages 10–17 rose from 4.7 per 100,000 in 2024 to 7.7 in 2025, mostly among boys (WA DOH, via KOMO). In King County, a University of Washington review found three-quarters of suicide deaths among children 10–14 involved a firearm, usually a family member's (coverage). Locked storage is the most protective step a household can take.

    If someone is struggling, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 24/7). Teens can reach Crisis Connections' Teen Link at 866-833-6546.

    Glossary

    What every score, measure and label on this site means, and how to read it when you're comparing schools. You can also hover or tap the i next to any heading.

    How it works

    Every number here comes from a public source, and every judgment comes from a rule you can read below. For what each measure means, see the Glossary.

    Which schools are included

    177 public and charter schools: all of the Renton, Kent, Issaquah and Bellevue school districts, plus Seattle Public Schools buildings in West Seattle (ZIPs 98106, 98116, 98126, 98136, 98146) and South Seattle (98108, 98118, 98144, 98178), plus four charters in those areas (Summit Atlas, Rainier Valley Leadership Academy, Impact Salish Sea and Impact Black River).

    The four scores

    Each score is a percentile from 0 to 100 that compares a school only with schools at the same level here (elementary with elementary, and so on). A 70 means the school did better than 70% of its peers in these six areas. It is not a grade and not a comparison with the whole state.

    • Academics: the average percentile of reading, math and science proficiency.
    • Growth: the average percentile of median student growth in reading and math. The state only measures growth in grades 4–8, so high schools have no Growth score.
    • Equity: the average percentile of three things: how far reading and math results sit above or below what the school's low-income share predicts, low-income students' own proficiency, and (where reported) how much more often students with disabilities are suspended than the school average, with lower being better.
    • Climate: the average percentile of regular attendance and a low suspension rate, plus the share of 9th graders on track for high schools.

    Not scored: special-education, re-engagement, institutional, online and preschool programs, and schools with fewer than 60 students. A score is blank when its inputs are missing.

    The "Reading this school" notes

    These come from fixed rules, not from editing by hand. Examples: results 8+ points above or below the income trend, median growth of 56+ or 44 and under, a 22-point reading gap between low-income students and the school, students with disabilities suspended at 2.5× the school rate or more, attendance under 65% or over 88%. The wording draws on research about school climate, belonging and exclusionary discipline. These are interpretations to start a conversation, not findings about a school.

    • Teachers and spending: OSPI teacher experience and per-pupil expenditure, 2024-25.

    • District context: U.S. Census Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates (2024, via the Urban Institute) and the WA DSHS Community Risk Profiles (King County summary, May 2024, the latest; DSHS's July 2026 district reports no longer include these ratings).     Reading the numbers

    • Suppression: OSPI hides small counts. "<3.7%" means somewhere between 0 and 3.7%. For scores and charts these are placed at the midpoint. "n<10" means too few students to report.
    • Discipline is the share of students suspended or expelled at least once. Low can mean calm, restorative or under-reported, so read it next to attendance.
    • Federal incident counts are self-reported and from 2023-24, with 2021-22, the first full year back in buildings, for comparison. A change between the two can reflect reporting practices as much as behavior. Use them to raise questions, not to rank safety.
    • Trends start in 2018-19 because no state tests were given in 2019-20 or 2020-21. Small schools get a caution when they have under 150 students or their results move 8+ points a year on average.
    • Support staff ratios divide this year's enrollment by the staff the school reported in 2023-24, so staffing may have changed since.
    • Healthy Youth Survey results are self-reports. Differences of a few points are often within the margin of error.
